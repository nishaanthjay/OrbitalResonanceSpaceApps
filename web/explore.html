    05Fires & weather

    Check the signal, then what else was going on

    Pick a possible clearing to see the fires detected near it.

    Fires near a spot don't prove they caused the change. The data is NASA's fire detections, downloaded afterwards.

    The example is simply the biggest patch on the watchlist. It was picked by size, so it isn't a representative sample for testing the method.

    Rainfall and soil moisture were not retrieved for this submission, so we say nothing about them. See what this can't tell you

    How we know

    The fire count uses NASA FIRMS detections. Each covers about 375 m. For the selected spot we count detections within 5 km of the patch centre and within 7 days before or after the radar date, and we use the provider's own confidence codes. A fire nearby doesn't mean fire caused the change.

    Rainfall (NASA rainfall data) and soil moisture (NASA soil-moisture data) were not retrieved for this submission. They're the next thing we plan to add (see web/tools/ENVIRONMENT.md).

    How we sort the evidence

    Possible clearings that match an alert keep the label they already had. They overlap a GLAD-L or GLAD-S2 deforestation alert between June 26 and November 5. We fixed that window in advance. Every other patch stays at "not enough evidence to confirm". We don't judge the photos from the regional share of clear sky. Calling a date wet or dry needs long-term records of normal weather, and we have none. There is no calibrated confidence probability (no "X% sure").

    The fire and weather context only covers the 60 km southern Pará box. Patches elsewhere in the full radar image have none. This part is kept apart from the detection and testing rules, which are frozen.

    How we know

    These are possible clearings (not confirmed). We found them with a Japanese radar method for spotting forest clearing. Open a patch to see it on the map. Its ground and tree signal history is drawn next to forest that didn't change.

    Long-wave radar passes through clouds. It measures the radar echo: the energy that bounces back off the ground and trees. We read it two ways: the ground signal and the tree signal.

    A ground signal going up can mean a clearing has just started. A tree signal going down can mean it's further along. But rain, fire and flooding change the signal too.

    Compare patches with GLAD-L deforestation alerts and look at their history. A possible clearing on radar is not the same as confirmed deforestation.

    Values for a single spot come from the map images, so they can hit the brightness limits. Patch lines use the full-precision radar data instead.

    08Limits

    What this can't tell you

    Agreeing with Hansen or GLAD means we match other maps. It is not proof we are right.

    What this can't tell you

    • Dry season only: we have June to October 2026. We did not test the wet season, when cloud is worst.
    • Possible clearings are not confirmed. Nobody has checked them on the ground.
    • Flags compare the last two passes with the first three, so many are likely seasonal drying, not clearing.
    • We use NISAR's early-release radar images, which may still change. The map layers are compressed to show on the web.
    • Hansen and GLAD are other maps, made from satellite photos. We compare with them, but they are not the final word.
    • Our first simple detector failed. The numbers are in "How we know" above.
    • We can't see selective logging (cutting only some trees), fire or flooding.

    We don't claim this stops deforestation, saves carbon, or works everywhere.

    Where it could go next

    Radar passes from the wet season and from more than one year. A fix for seasonal changes. A method that also watches pasture that was cleared before. And labels from people outside the project.
