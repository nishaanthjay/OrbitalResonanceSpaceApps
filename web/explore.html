Amazon Forest WatchThe Next Frontier.

For much of the rainy season, clouds hide the Amazon from photo satellites. NISAR, a radar satellite, sees through them. Behind this text is NISAR's view: its tree-signal image. Forest looks bright and cleared land looks dark. We tested what it catches and what it misses, and we show you both.

    Fires / NASA FIRMS

    Hot spots nearby

    Checking data

    Each NASA fire detection covers about 375 m. We look within 5 km of the patch centre and ±7 days. We use the detection times and the provider's own confidence codes. A fire nearby doesn't mean fire caused the change.

    FIRMS access and metadata

    Rainfall (NASA rainfall data) and soil moisture (NASA soil-moisture data) were not retrieved for this submission. They're the next thing we plan to add (see web/tools/ENVIRONMENT.md).

    How we sort the evidence

    Possible clearings that match an alert keep the label they already had. They overlap a GLAD-L or GLAD-S2 deforestation alert between June 26 and November 5. We fixed that window in advance. Every other patch stays at "not enough evidence to confirm". We don't judge the photos from the regional share of clear sky. Calling a date wet or dry needs long-term records of normal weather, and we have none. There is no calibrated confidence probability (no "X% sure").

    The fire and weather context only covers the 60 km southern Pará box. Patches elsewhere in the full radar image have none. This part is kept apart from the detection and testing rules, which are frozen.

    01 / Look

    Watch the trees

    Long-wave radar passes through clouds. It measures the radar echo: the energy that bounces back off the ground and trees. We read it two ways: the ground signal and the tree signal.

    02 / Spot

    Spot a change

    A ground signal going up can mean a clearing has just started. A tree signal going down can mean it's further along. But rain, fire and flooding change the signal too.

    Reading a patch's history

    Pick a patch and the radar map zooms to its outline. Its ground and tree signal lines show up in the controls panel.

    • Ground signal (HH) solid line: the spot or patch you picked.
    • Tree signal (HV) solid line: when land is cleared, it usually drops away from the forest line.
    • Dashed lines: forest that didn't change, for scale.

    Values for a single spot come from the map images, so they can hit the brightness limits. Patch lines use the full-precision radar data instead.

    08 / How good is it

    How good is it, and its limits

    When we agree with Hansen or GLAD, we match other maps. That is not proof we are right. Nobody has checked our map on the ground. Every flag is a possible clearing, not confirmed.

    Possible clearings vs deforestation alerts

    How many possible-clearing squares overlap a GLAD alert (±24 days)? We compare that with the same number of random squares of unchanged forest. A higher ratio means the flags aren't random. This is not accuracy, and most flags have no alert at all.

    Limits

    • Dry season only: we have June to October 2026. We didn't test the wet season, when cloud is worst.
    • Flags compare the last two passes with the first three, so many are likely seasonal drying, not clearing.
    • We use NISAR's early-release radar images, which may still change. The map layers are compressed.
    • Hansen and GLAD are made from satellite photos. We compare with them, but they are not the final word.
    • We can't spot selective logging (cutting only some trees), fire or flooding.
    • Our first try, a simple change detector (1 dB), failed. Details are above.

    We don't claim this stops deforestation, saves carbon, or works everywhere.

    Where it could go next

    Radar passes from the wet season and from more than one year. A fix for seasonal changes. A method that also watches pasture that was cleared before. And labels from people outside the project.
